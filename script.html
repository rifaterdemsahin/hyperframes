03 · The goal

Here's the goal. Company debt stays the company's. Liquidation hands the company to a licensed practitioner, who sells the assets and pays creditors in order. Generally, the director isn't personally liable.

04 · Five things to do first

Five things to do first. One: check your HMRC balance. Mine is £39,475.54. Two: gather the company's books and records. Mine are in a storage unit, and I'm only paying for it until I get access to the documents. Three: get a licensed insolvency practitioner. I chose one with 4.8 stars on Trustpilot. Four: stop trading, and don't pay one creditor ahead of the others. Your practitioner will confirm this. Five: close your invoices and bring every bank account to zero, or the money goes to the Crown. I've recovered both outstanding customer invoices, £2,203.17 in total, and that's everything I received from my customers.

05 · What it costs and how long it takes

Here's what it costs, and how long it takes. A £1,500 deposit, then a £2,100 balance. I've paid both. There are five stages: the deposit, the information pack, the board meeting, the liquidator taking over, and finally the banks at zero and the Gazette notice, followed by a wait of about two months. In my case, I signed Stage 3 on 30 October, the creditors are notified on 19 November, and the proposed liquidation date is 2 December.

06 · The exceptions

Now the exceptions, because this matters. The protection isn't absolute. A director can still be personally liable for wrongful or fraudulent trading, for personal guarantees, for an overdrawn director's loan account, and for paying one creditor ahead of the others. That's why the practitioner decides, not me.
